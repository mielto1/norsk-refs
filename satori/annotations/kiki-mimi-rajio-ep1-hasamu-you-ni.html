X Dictionary Entryそのそのthat (something or someone distant from the speaker, close to the listener; actions of the listener, or ideas expressed or understood by the listener); the [adj. used directly before noun]Add to Your StudylistDelete Other Note そのそのひろば広場ひろばををはさ挟はさむむようようにに Hasamu is one of those verbs that can feel really tricky to get your head around. It's used way more frequently than you might expect from the dictionary definition of "to interpose," so let's take a look together and nail down how it works. The key to understanding hasamu is to think "sandwich." When you hasamu something, you sandwich it. You stick it between two other things. There are different ways to use the verb, but the constant is that the object of hasamu is the meat in your sandwich. Let's try it out with a literal sandwich. パンパンででハムハムををはさ挟はさんでんでた食たべたべた。I sandwiched ham with bread and ate it. Now, no one would use this sentence, just as no one would say, "I held ham between two pieces of bread and ate it," to which the only sane reply is, "You mean you had a ham sandwich?" However, it nicely illustrates one pattern. The object (the meat, the thing being sandwiched) is marked by wo. The things used to do the sandwiching (the bread, in this case) are marked with de. Now let's try a slightly different variation. In the above sentence, the ham is a victim who waits tremblingly as two slices of bread surround it. In other words, the ham is stationary; what the doer of the verb manipulates is the bread. You can get an idea of this by noting that the bread is marked with de. The two slices are tools used to achieve the sandwiching effect. Make sense? Good. You can also use hasamu with a focus on movement that happens to the meat. In other words, you can say that you hasamu meat into bread. The end result is the same, and the meat is still the object, but this time we see the bread as stationary, with the meat sliding into the middle of it. As you might guess, in this pattern, the bread is now a destination, not a tool, so it gets marked with ni. Take a look: パンパンににハムハムををはさ挟はさんでんでた食たべたべた。I stuck ham between (two slices of) bread and ate it. Again, the key difference is: Are you using the bread as a tool to squeeze the meat (pan de)? Or are you injecting the meat into the waiting bread (pan ni)? This pattern can be used to speak of "sticking" or "wedging" something into something else. Let's graduate one level and try one that is not literally about a sandwich: けいじ刑事けいじははタバコタバコををゆび指ゆびににはさ挟はさんでんで、そと外そとををなが眺ながめためた。The detective gazed outside with a cigarette between his fingers. Here the detective hasamu's the cigarette into his fingers. He sticks it there. One more: かれ彼かれははてがみ手紙てがみををほん本ほんににはさ挟はさんでんで、へや部屋へやににもど戻もどったった。He stuck the letter into a book and returned to his room. You might wonder how we know if there's just one book or two. It could be either. It really depends on what is the more natural interpretation. In the case of hasamu'ing into fingers, it's hard to imagine a cigarette being inserted into the middle of a single finger, so wedging it between two is the only natural interpretation. In contrast, it's pretty common to stick flat things (bookmarks, etc) into a book. If you wanted to make it clear that the letter was between two books, not inside of one, you could say hon to hon no aida ni hasanda, "he sandwiched it between book and book," or something like that. So far, so good? Okay. Where hasamu can get tricky is when the "bread" of the sandwich is not named. Try this one on for size: ふたり二人ふたりははテーブルテーブルををはさ挟はさんでんですわ座すわったった。The two sandwiched a table (Not named: with what? Answer: their bodies) and sat. = The two sat across from each other at a table. This kind of sentence is super common, so read it a few times and make sure it makes sense to you. The two sat in such a way that they sandwiched a table. Here's another pattern that made me go cross-eyed the first hundred times I heard it: どうろ道路どうろををはさ挟はさんでんでむ向むかいかいがわ側がわののビルビルははぎんこう銀行ぎんこうですです。The building on the opposite side of the street, with the street in between (us and it), is a bank. If you feel woozy, just literally translate douro wo hasande as "sandwiching the road." That gives us, "Sandwiching the road, the building on the opposite side is a bank." Take your time and break this down slowly. The meat of the sandwich is the road, and the bread is the bank and...(wait for it)...us! Now, at last, we can analyze the sentence from the article. The highlighted phrase means "in such a way as to sandwich the square." The narrator is about to tell us about the two slices of bread that sandwich (=are on either side of) the square: tennis courts and a forest area.
