You might have noticed that Suzuki calls Sakura by her given name with no suffix, while Sakura calls Suzuki by his family name plus *-kun*. What's going on here?

There is quite a bit of variation in how couples address each other, and a lot of it has to do with how they first met. For example, if a woman works with a *Suzuki-kun* at the office and later starts dating him, he will not suddenly become *Tarou-kun* just because they went on a date or two. He's probably going to stay *Suzuki-kun* for a while -- that's just who he is to her. Perhaps something similar is happening in the case of Sakura.

But what about Suzuki? Why does he call Sakura by her first name only? The practice of dropping the suffixes and just calling someone by their unadorned name (either first or last) is called:

[example block: Japanese line, then a dotted rule, then the English line]

> 呼(よ)び捨(す)て
>
> "Calling and throwing away" (calling someone by their name with no suffix)

Using *yobi-sute* sounds very close, like the way a parent might refer to their own child. It can also sound authoritative, like the way a boss might address a subordinate. There is a certain tough-guy, "no politeness given," quality to it. (So, just to be clear, this will feel **extremely rude** if the other party does not consider you to have the right to call them that way.)

Because of this rough-and-tough, intimate circle quality, some young men in settings such as school and work call their peers this way.

One of our writers here at Satori Reader was called by her last name with no suffix by a classmate when she was in high school, and even after they started dating later, he continued calling her that way for years. That was just who she was to him.

On the other end of the spectrum, some couples call each other by their given names plus さん. This could happen because they met as adults in a neutral setting, and they simply never changed the way they addressed each other. One of this annotator's host families from Japan has been married for more than 30 years and they both still call each other with さん.

Even among married couples, the style is not always symmetrical. This annotator has a friend who calls his wife by her first name with no suffix. She calls him a nickname plus *-kun*.

All this demonstrates that the way that people call each other is **varied** and **specific to their individual relationship** with each other.
